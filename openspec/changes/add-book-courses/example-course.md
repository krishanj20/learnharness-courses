## How a chapter feels

**Placement preview.** Suppose a learner has independently explained Sharpe ratio and volatility, but has never reasoned about capacity. Chapter 3 says **Cover** and highlights §3.4, alongside the evidence for the familiar sections. If earlier answers also demonstrate expected return and capacity, the chapter can say **Skip** and explain why. These are example learner profiles. A personal plan needs the learner’s own evidence.

**First reading.** Open Chapter 1. The first assignment reads “Read §1.1, The Securities, printed pp. 7–9 (PDF pp. 35–37). Stop at §1.2, Modes of Exchange.” The next heading is halfway down printed p. 9, so both assignments include that page. Selecting **I’ve read this section** starts the discussion in the same chat.

**First question.** “Two investors want different contract terms. Why might they both accept a standard contract?” If the learner is stuck, ask “Which contract would be easier to find another buyer for?” After the discussion, assign §1.2. The chapter then proceeds through §1.3 on participants, §1.4 on excess returns and §1.5 on the elements of quantitative investing. Each section gets its own reading prompt. The opening chapter questions and closing takeaways inform a tutor-written exam for this chapter.

**An exam with source exercises.** Exercise 2.1 is on printed p. 71 (PDF p. 99). It asks the learner to establish a bound connecting compounded and arithmetic returns. After “don’t know”, ask “If you start with £1, what do you have after two successive periods with returns of 10% and −10%?” Then ask which single constant return would produce that final wealth. Return to the general inequality. A tutor-derived rubric can use AM–GM on the nonnegative gross returns, including equality when all are equal. Label it as tutor-derived unless the importer locates a matching published solution.
